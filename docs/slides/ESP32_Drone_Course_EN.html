02

How a quadcopter flies

Four props, three axes, and why it cannot fly without a computer.

Section 2 has no code at all. It builds the picture you need to understand every line of firmware later: thrust, the three rotation axes, and why a quadcopter is unstable on its own.

How it flies

A quadcopter is four fans pushing air down

Each prop throws air down. The air pushes the prop up with the same force: thrust.

Faster prop → more air per second → more thrust on that corner.

No moving surfaces: unlike a plane, every move comes from changing four motor speeds.

Example

A rowing boat: the oar pushes water back, the boat goes forward.

How it flies · 8

This is Newton’s third law: push air down, get pushed up. The key idea for the whole course is in the middle card: a quadcopter has no flaps or rudder. Every movement — up, tilt, turn — comes from making some motors faster than others. So the firmware’s whole job is choosing four motor speeds.

How it flies

Thrust against weight decides up or down

Up or down is a tug-of-war between thrust and weight.

Throttle up → climbs. Throttle in the middle → hovers. Throttle down → sinks.

As the battery drains, the same setting gives less thrust — so a fixed setting slowly sinks.

Example

A 40 g drone needs about 40 g of thrust to hover: roughly 10 g from each motor (illustration).

How it flies · 9

The last sentence explains a behaviour you will meet later: the Hold mode keeps a thrust setting, not a height. With no barometer the drone cannot know its height, so as the battery voltage drops, the same thrust setting lifts less and the drone sinks slowly. Why can’t the firmware simply add more thrust over time? Because it would not know how much — it cannot measure height.

How it flies

Three ways to rotate: roll, pitch, yaw

Three ways to rotate, plus throttle.

Roll moves the drone sideways.

Pitch moves it forward or back.

Yaw changes where the nose points.

Plus throttle for up and down: four controls, and one PID controller per rotation.

How it flies · 10

Memory trick: roll = a wing dips, pitch = a nod, yaw = a head shake. In the code you will see three of everything: three gyro rates (gyro_roll, gyro_pitch, gyro_yaw), three PID controllers and three outputs. Throttle is handled separately because it is not a rotation.

How it flies

Two props spin one way, two spin the other

A spinning prop twists the drone the other way. Mixing directions cancels the twist.

Clockwise: front-right (RF) and rear-left (LR).

Counter-clockwise: rear-right (RR) and front-left (LF).

Each pair sits on a diagonal, so the twists cancel and the drone stays balanced.

How it flies · 16

A human pilot cannot react 250 times a second. The firmware must sense every small tilt and correct it before it grows. That is the main job of the flight controller.

How it flies

The fix is feedback

Example: keeping a car in its lane. You see the drift, compare it to the lane centre, and turn the wheel a little — over and over.

1

Measure what the drone is doing now (the motion sensor).

2

Compare it to what we want (the setpoint from the stick or the app).

3

Correct: change the motors by an amount based on the difference (the error).

4

Repeat — fast enough that errors stay small.

How it flies · 17

This is closed-loop control. The difference between want and have is called the error. PID, which you meet in section 6, is one recipe for turning the error into a correction.

Each step is a function in loop(): mpuReadRaw, attitudeUpdate, pidCompute, motorMix and motorWrite, then a busy-wait until 4 ms have passed. We will walk through every one.

How it flies

Why 250 times a second?

4 ms

per loop (LOOP_TIME_US 4000). Short enough to catch a tilt before it grows.

250 Hz

the sensor sample rate is set to match: 1 kHz ÷ (1 + 3).

≈ 0.5°

an example drift between two loops — small, so each correction is small.

Rule of thumb: the loop must run much faster than the drone can tip over.

How it flies · 19 · Source: Esp32_drone.ino #define LOOP_TIME_US, mpuInit() SMPLRT_DIV

Why not faster? The sensor is set to 250 Hz samples, the radios and logging need time, and the brushed motors cannot respond faster anyway. Why not slower? Corrections would come late and would have to be bigger, which makes the drone wobble. The 0.5 degree figure is an illustration.

Accelerometer

Hardware · 26 · Source: Esp32_drone.ino #define PIN_SCK / PIN_MISO / PIN_MOSI / PIN_MPU_CS

SPI is a fast, simple bus: the master (ESP32) drives the clock and the slave (MPU6500) answers. This firmware runs it at 1 MHz in SPI mode 0. Why SPI and not I2C? SPI is faster and has no addressing, so a 14-byte read takes very little of the 4 ms budget.

A hobby radio receiver can plug in on GPIO 4 and fly the drone with a real transmitter.

One signal wire

16 channels packed into one 25-byte frame.

Unusual serial

100000 baud, 8 data bits, even parity, 2 stop bits, inverted.

The safety net

SBUS always flies in manual rate mode. Section 8 decodes it.

Hardware · 27 · Source: Esp32_drone.ino sbusInit()

Why is SBUS inverted? It was designed by Futaba with an inverted signal. The ESP32 UART can invert in hardware, so the firmware passes true as the fifth argument of Serial2.begin. No inverter chip is needed.

Hardware

AO3416 MOSFET: the motor switch

A GPIO pin cannot power a motor. A MOSFET is an electronic switch that can.

Small signal in

The ESP32 pin drives the gate with 3.3 V and almost no current.

Big current through

The MOSFET lets battery current flow through the motor.

Fast

It can switch on and off 20,000 times a second.

Example

Like a light switch you flip with one finger that controls a big lamp.

Why this part? It is a small N-channel MOSFET that turns fully on with a low gate voltage, so a 3.3 V ESP32 pin can drive it directly. One MOSFET per motor: Q1 to Q4 on the schematic.

Reading and smoothing the battery

uint32_t mv = analogReadMilliVolts(PIN_VBAT);
float sample = (mv / 1000.0f) * VBAT_DIVIDER;
if (vbat <= 0.0f) vbat = sample;        // first read
else vbat += VBAT_FILTER_ALPHA * (sample - vbat);

analogReadMilliVolts uses the chip's factory calibration.

Alpha 0.02: each loop moves 2% toward the new reading — about a 0.2 s time constant.

Why smooth? One reading is noisy and dips with every throttle punch.

Example

Old value 3.90 V, one noisy sample 3.60 V → new value 3.90 + 0.02 × (−0.30) = 3.894 V. One bad reading barely moves it.

Hardware · 33 · Source: Esp32_drone.ino batteryUpdate()

This is a first-order low-pass filter, also called an exponential moving average. Example: the old value is 3.90 V and a noisy sample reads 3.60 V. The new value is 3.90 + 0.02 × (−0.30) = 3.894 V. One bad sample barely moves it, but a real drop shows up within a fraction of a second.

Firmware architecture

One Arduino sketch, one loop, and the order things happen in.

Section 4 is the map of the code. Before we zoom into any function, we look at how the 2,600-line sketch is organised and how data moves through one 4 ms loop.

Architecture

One sketch, two entry points

Every Arduino program has the same shape. Esp32_drone.ino is no different.

setup()

Runs once at power-on. Starts the hardware, loads saved settings, calibrates the gyro.

loop()

Runs forever, once every 4 ms. Reads, decides, drives the motors.

No RTOS tasks

The flight code does not create its own threads. One loop, one order.

Example

Like a cook following one recipe card from top to bottom, again and again, instead of several cooks working at once.

Architecture · 42 · Source: Esp32_drone.ino setup(), loop()

Why no separate tasks? Many flight controllers use an RTOS with tasks for sensors, control and radio. This firmware keeps everything in one loop so a learner can read it top to bottom and know exactly what runs when. The Wi-Fi and Bluetooth stacks still run in the background inside the ESP32 core.

Architecture

What setup() does, in order

1

motorInit() and motorStop() — motors off before anything else.

2

mpuInit() — if WHO_AM_I is wrong, print a wiring hint and stop forever.

3

ADC attenuation for the battery pin, then sbusInit().

4

Open NVS and cfgLoad() — saved PID gains and flight settings.

Hard 250 Hz pacing

// ---- hard 250 Hz pacing ----
while (micros() - loop_timer < LOOP_TIME_US) { /* spin */ }
loop_timer = micros();

Busy-wait: the CPU spins until exactly 4 ms have passed since the last loop.

Why exact timing? The gyro is integrated with a fixed dt of 0.004 s. A loop of the wrong length gives the wrong angle.

Wrap-safe: unsigned subtraction still works when micros() overflows after ~71 minutes.

Example

The work takes 1.3 ms this loop → the spin lasts 2.7 ms. Next loop the work takes 2.1 ms → the spin lasts 1.9 ms. The period stays 4 ms.

Architecture · 48 · Source: Esp32_drone.ino end of loop()

Why not use delay()? delay() waits a fixed time no matter how long the loop work took, so the period would change with the workload. The busy-wait absorbs the variable work time. The cost: if the work ever takes longer than 4 ms, the loop runs late and dt is wrong for that loop.

Talking to a register over SPI

// write: reg & 0x7F (bit7 = 0), then the value

CS low starts the conversation; CS high ends it.

PID control

Turning “how wrong are we?” into “how hard do we push?”

Section 6 is the heart of the flight controller. PID is a simple recipe with three parts. The firmware uses it three times: roll, pitch and yaw.

PID control

PID in one analogy: a shower

P looks at now, I at the past, D at the future.

P only: pushes hard, overshoots, wobbles — and stops a little short.

P + D: D brakes the swing, so it is calm — still a little short.

P + I + D: I removes the last gap, so it settles on the target.

Every PID controller is just these three reactions added together: output = P + I + D. The skill of tuning is choosing how strong each reaction is — the three gains Kp, Ki and Kd.

PID control

P: push in proportion to the error

P = Kp × error. Double the error, double the push.

Roll and pitch Kp

1.3

Yaw Kp

4.0 (yaw torque is weak)

Too much P

The drone shakes fast (oscillates). Too little: it feels soft and lazy.

Example

The drone rolls at 10 °/s but we want 0 → error 10 → P = 1.3 × 10 = 13 units of motor correction.

P alone usually leaves a small steady error, because once the error is small, the push is small too. That is the job of I. P alone also tends to overshoot, which is the job of D.

I fixes the error that will not go away.

I adds up the error every loop: i_mem += Ki × error.

A small error that lasts becomes a big correction.

Roll and pitch Ki = 0.04 — tiny per loop, but 250 loops a second.

Example

Battery 2 mm off-centre → error 1 °/s. After 1 s: I = 0.04 × 1 × 250 = 10 units — enough to hold level.

Why does a drone need I? Real drones are never perfectly balanced: the battery sits a little off-centre, one motor is a little weaker. P would leave a small constant drift. I keeps growing until it cancels that imbalance exactly.

PID control

D: brake before you overshoot

D reacts to how fast the error is changing. It is a damper.

Code

D = Kd × (error − last error).

Roll and pitch Kd

18 — large, because the change per 4 ms loop is small.

Too much D

Hot motors and a high-pitched buzz: D amplifies sensor noise.

Example

Error goes from 10 to 7 in one loop → change −3 → D = 18 × (−3) = −54, braking the correction before it overshoots.

Why no D on yaw (Kd 0)? Yaw changes slowly and its torque is weak, so it rarely overshoots, and D would only add noise. Why is D so sensitive to noise? Noise changes quickly from loop to loop, and D multiplies every quick change by 18.
